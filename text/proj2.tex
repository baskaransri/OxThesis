\section{Introduction}
In this chapter, we focus on sampling and reconstruction in the context of another downstream task, specifically binary classification with linearized GCNs. We theoretically characterise the relationship between reconstruction error and classification task error, and derive a sampling scheme for reconstruction and classifying with a linearized GCN. We empirically demonstrate how sampling methods which are optimal for reconstruction performance can underperform random sampling when the reconstruction is used for binary classification, and we compare the performance of our novel sampling method on both synthetic and real-world node classification datasets.

\section{Related work}
\subsection{Sampling and reconstruction in graph signal processing}
Graph signal processing generalises the tools of sampling and reconstruction from classical signal processing by extending the classical shift operator to a graph shift operator \cite{ortega2018graph} such as the Laplacian. The majority of studies on graph-based sampling focus on designing efficient sampling schemes for criteria which approximate \cite{wang2018optimal, wang2019low, mfn, tremblay2017graph, jayawant2021doptimal,puy2018random} or bound \cite{bai2020fast,eoptimalchen} reconstruction loss, grounded in the optimal design of experiments \cite{pukelsheim2006optimal}. Such schemes optimizing mean squared reconstruction error are called `A-optimal'.

As these works focus on reconstruction error, the theoretical results apply specifically to this setting -- for example, the results on the near-optimality of greedy sampling \cite{chamon2016near} depend on the (approximate) supermodularity of the specific loss, and so do not immediately apply to the classification setting. Except for results on perfect reconstruction in the noiseless setting \cite{pesenson2008sampling} (which would imply perfect classification in our setting), results on the performance of reconstruction in the broader setting \cite{puy2018random,chamon2017greedy,shomorony2014sampling} do not immediately carry over to the classification setting.

\subsection{Graph ML tasks in presence of missing data}
Graph Machine Learning takes two approaches to the problem of missing data -- feature imputation and label prediction. Feature imputation involves reconstructing the graph signal, and such methods include classical matrix completion methods \cite{candes2012exact,cai2010singular} and autoencoder-based methods \cite{spinelli2020missing}, which also can tackle issues around missing edges. Label prediction involves directly predicting the class, such as Label Propagation \cite{zhou2003learning} or directly using GNNs on the partial data with adjusted message passing \cite{jiang2020incomplete, taguchi2021graph}. Some approaches combine the two \cite{you2020handling}. We note that Feature Propagation (FP) \cite{rossi2021unreasonable} makes inroads into bridging the Graph ML approach and the GSP approach by doing imputation motivated by a Graph Laplacian-based criterion while evaluating the effectiveness under a more complex downstream task.

While work in graph ML engages with missing data in settings with significantly more complex downstream tasks than graph signal processing, it has done so through an empirical lens -- evaluating the performance of their methods on specific datasets -- and thus there is a dearth of theoretical results. \section{Theoretical Results and Implications}
\subsection{General Results}
\label{sec:Gen_Theory}
%In this section, we present results for features drawn from a zero-mean distribution. 
We begin with a \mccorrect{presentation of Sheppard's lemma, which is core to later proofs in this chapter}:

\begin{lemma}
\label{lemma:gaussian_sign}
Consider \bs{$X,Y$ real scalar random variables with} $\binom{X}{Y} \sim \mathcal{N}\left( \vect{0}, \begin{psmallmatrix} \phi^{2} & c \\ c & \nu^{2} \end{psmallmatrix} \right)$ with $\phi, \nu, c \in \mathbb{R}$. Let $\rho = \text{Corr}(X,Y) = \frac{c}{\phi\nu}$. Then
\begin{equation}
    %\mathbb{P}\left( X \text{ and } Y \text{ have different signs}\right) = \frac{ \text{arccos }\rho }{\pi}.
     \bs{\prob{\text{sign}(X) \neq \text{sign}(Y) }} = \frac{ \text{arccos }\rho }{\pi}.
\end{equation}
    
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    \mccorrect{The original proof can be found in Sheppard's 1899 paper} \cite{sheppard1899iii}\mccorrect{; we present an alternative proof in Appendix }\ref{app:proof_sheppard}.
\end{proof}

We now characterise our losses for a class of linear $f$. This class of $f$ includes linearized GCNs (see Sec \ref{sec:experimental_setup} for a specific example). Our result also specialises to an extension of the classical GSP sampling and reconstruction setting, which we discuss after the theorem.
    
\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:general_class_err}
    If the function $f$, which maps from the input features to the final-layer representation, can be written as $$f(\matr{X}) = \matr{G}\matr{X}\matr{w}$$ where $\matr{G}\in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$ and $\vect{w} \in \mathbb{R}^{d} \backslash \{\vect{0}\}$, then for a sample set $\set{S}$,
    \begin{align}
         \text{Classification Loss}  = \sum_{i \in \set{V}} \frac{1}{\pi}\text{arccos}\left( \rho_{i}(\matr{G}) \right) \label{eq:gen_class_err}
        \end{align}
    and if $d=1$,
        \begin{align}
        \text{Reconstruction Loss}  =  \sum_{i \in \set{V}} \left(\phi_{i}(\matr{I})\right)^{2} + \left(\nu_{i}(\matr{I})\right)^{2} - 2c_{i}(\matr{I}) \label{eq:gen_rec_err}
        %\sum_{i \in \set{V}} \frac{1}{\pi}\text{arccos}\left( \frac{\left(\matr{R}_{\set{S}}\matrsub[S,N]{\Sigma}\right)_{ii}}{\sqrt{\matr{\Sigma}_{ii}\left(\matr{R}_{\set{S}}\left(\matr{\Sigma} + n^{2}\matr{I}_{N}\right) \matr{R}_{\set{S}}^{T} \right)_{ii}}} \right)
    \end{align}
    where for any matrix $\matr{M}$, we let
    \begin{align}
        \matr{C} &= \text{Cov}(\matr{X}\vect{w}) \cdot (\sqnormvec{\vect{w}})^{-1},\\
        c_{i}(\matr{M}) &={\left(\matr{M}\matr{R}_{\set{S}}\matrsub[S,N]{C} \matr{M}^{T}\right)_{ii}}, \\
        \left(\phi_{i}(\matr{M})\right)^{2} &= \left(\matr{M}\matr{C}\matr{M}^{T}\right)_{ii}, \\
        \left(\nu_{i}(\matr{M})\right)^{2} &= \left( \matr{M}\matr{R}_{\set{S}}\msubgen[\set{S}]{\matr{C} + \sigma^{2}\matr{I}_{N}} \matr{R}_{\set{S}}^{T} \matr{M}^{T}\right)_{ii},\\
        \rho_{i}\left(\matr{M}\right) &= \frac{c_{i}(\matr{M})}{\phi_{i}(\matr{M})\nu_{i}(\matr{M})}.
    \end{align}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    %For each node, $\expect{f(\matr{X})} = \mathbb{E}[{f(\hat{\matr{X}})}] = 0$ so 
    As $\matr{X}$ is independent to the noise and $\expect{\matr{X}}=0$,
    \begin{align}
        \text{Cov}(f(\hat{\vect{X}})_{i}, f({\vect{X}})_{i}) &= \expect{f(\hat{\vect{X}})f({\vect{X}})^{T}}_{ii} \\
        &= \matr{G}\matr{R}_{\set{S}}\matrsub[S,N]{I}\expect{(\matr{X} + \sigma \cdot \vect{\epsilon})\vect{w}\vect{w}^{T}\matr{X}^{T}}\matr{G}^{T} \\
        &= \matr{G}\matr{R}_{\set{S}}\msubgen[\set{S},\set{N}]{\expect{\matr{X} \vect{w}\vect{w}^{T}\matr{X}^{T}}}\matr{G}^{T} \\
        &= \matr{G}\matr{R}_{\set{S}}\msubgen[\set{S},\set{N}]{\text{Cov}(\matr{X}\vect{w})}\matr{G}^{T} \\
        &= \sqnormvec{\vect{w}}c_{i}(\matr{G}).
    \end{align}
    Similarly, $\text{Var}(f(\matr{X})_{i}) = \sqnormvec{\vect{w}}(\phi_{i}(\matr{G}))^{2}$ and  $\text{Var}(f(\hat{\matr{X}})_{i}) = \sqnormvec{\vect{w}}(\nu_{i}(\matr{G}))^{2}$ giving $\rho_{i}(\matr{G}) = \text{Corr}(f(\hat{\vect{X}})_{i}, f({\vect{X}})_{i})$. For classification loss, as $(f(\hat{\vect{X}})_{i}$ and $f({\vect{X}})_{i})$ are linear transformations of $\matr{X}$, they are jointly Gaussian and we can apply Lemma \ref{lemma:gaussian_sign} to get that the misclassification probability at node $i$ is $\frac{\text{arccos}(\rho_{i}(\matr{G}))}{\pi}$; sum across all nodes to get the total loss as described in \eqref{eq:gen_class_err}. 
    
    For the Reconstruction loss, if $d=1$,
    \begin{align}
     \expect{||\matr{X} - \hat{\matr{X}}||^{2}_{F}} &=  \expect{\text{tr}(\matr{X}\matr{X}^{T})} + \expect{\text{tr}(\hat{\matr{X}}\hat{\matr{X}}^{T})} - 2\expect{\text{tr}(\hat{\matr{X}}\matr{X}^{T})}  \\
     &= \text{tr}\left(\expect{\matr{X}\matr{X}^{T}}\right) + \text{tr}\left(\expect{\hat{\matr{X}}\hat{\matr{X}}^{T}}\right) - 2 \text{tr}\left(\expect{\hat{\matr{X}}\matr{X}^{T}} \right) \\
     &= \text{tr}\left(\matr{C}\right) + \text{tr}\left( \matr{R}_{\set{S}}\msubgen[\set{S}]{\matr{C} + \sigma^{2}\matr{I}_{N}} \matr{R}_{\set{S}}^{T} \right) - 2 \text{tr}\left(\matr{R}_{\set{S}}\matrsub[S,N]{C}\right) \label{eq:variance_calc_zero_mean}\\
     &= \sum_{i \in \set{V}} \matr{C}_{ii} + \left( \matr{R}_{\set{S}}\msubgen[\set{S}]{\matr{C} + \sigma^{2}\matr{I}_{N}} \matr{R}_{\set{S}}^{T} \right)_{ii} - 2 \left(\matr{R}_{\set{S}}\matrsub[S,N]{C}\right)_{ii} \\
     &=  \sum_{i \in \set{V}} \left(\phi_{i}(\matr{I})\right)^{2} + \left(\nu_{i}(\matr{I})\right)^{2} - 2c_{i}(\matr{I})
     %&= \sum_{i \in \set{V}} \text{Var}(\matr{X}_{i}) + \text{Var}(\hat{\matr{X}}_{i}) - 2\text{Cov}(\hat{\matr{X}}_{i}, \matr{X}_{i}) \\
    \end{align}
   where we use $\expect{\matr{X}} = \expect{\hat{\matr{X}}} = 0$ to compute \eqref{eq:variance_calc_zero_mean}. 
\end{proof}

% Note that classification loss is only dependent on $\matr{G}$, not $\vect{w}$.

 To interpret Theorem \ref{thm:general_class_err}, we first consider any $\matr{G}$ and LS reconstruction of a noise-free $k$-bandlimited signal; specifically $\matr{\Sigma} = \projbl$. In this case, $\sqrt{c_{i}(\matr{G})} = \nu_{i}(\matr{G})$ and the {\text{classification loss}} is $\sum_{i \in \set{V}} \frac{1}{\pi}\arccos{\left(\sqrt{\frac{c_{i}}{\phi_{i}^{2}}}\right)}$ where $0 \leq \frac{c_{i}}{\phi_{i}^{2}} \leq 1$. Thus, we expect the behaviour of classification \bs{loss} as it changes with sample size to look similar to $x \to \arccos{\left(\sqrt{x}\right)}$. 
 
 We consider the application of Theorem \ref{thm:general_class_err} to certain classes of $\matr{G}$ and their implications.

\paragraph{GSP Extension}As a special case, if we consider $d=1$ and let  $\matr{G} = \matr{I}$, we get an extension of the standard GSP sampling and reconstruction theory to the classification setting. In this case, the losses are both transformations of $\phi_{i}(\matr{I}), \nu_{i}(\matr{I})$ and $c_{i}(\matr{I})$, allowing us to more directly compare the two losses. This is not immediately possible in the case of general $\matr{G}${, thus this `simple' case is a helpful experimental setup for understanding the relationship between the two losses.}

\paragraph{GNNs} If we let $\matr{G}$ be a polynomial $p$ of $\tilde{\matr{A}}_{\gamma}$, then $\vect{l} = \text{sign}(p(\tilde{\matr{A}}_{\gamma})\matr{X}\matr{w})$, which corresponds to a multi-layer SGC \cite{wu2019simplifying} (see Section \ref{sec:GNN_background}) or a single layer polynomial-based GNN\footnote{We assume that all feature dimensions share the same polynomial filter.} like Chebnet \cite{defferrard2016convolutional}, FavardGNN \cite{guo2023Favard}, JacobiNet \cite{guo2023manipulating} or BernNet \cite{he2021bernnet}. Then the classification loss in Theorem \ref{thm:general_class_err} is the difference in output when applying the GNN to the full data $\matr{X}$ versus to the reconstructed data $\hat{\matr{X}}$. Note that in the case where the columns of $\matr{X}$ are independent, $\matr{C} = \matr{\Sigma}$, and therefore for SGC the classification loss does not depend on the weights, only on the number of layers.

\subsection{Relationship between Reconstruction and Classification Loss}
We now relate the reconstruction and classification losses via the error in reconstructing the output of $f$. The following Corollary holds for any value of $d$.

\label{eq:bound_final_layer}
\sum_{i \in \set{V}} \text{Error}_{out,i} \leq ||\matr{G}||^{2} \cdot \text{Reconstruction Loss}
\end{equation}
and for every node $i$, the following is always a valid triangle.

\subsection{Sampling for Classification}
\label{sec:optimal_sampling_design}
In previous chapters, we have focused on optimality criteria related to reconstruction loss, which in Chapter \ref{ch:2-litreview} we have related to various objectives (MMSE, WMSE, Confidence Ellipsoid) -- indeed, the optimality criteria can be derived from the given objectives through mathematical analysis of particular Gaussian models. We have also noted that these optimality criteria are purely focused on reconstruction, and do not take the downstream task into account. In this vein, rather than focusing on MMSE or WMSE, we can use our theoretical results to propose a new sampling scheme based on our theoretical characterisation of classification error. We call this a classification-optimal scheme.

We use Theorem \ref{thm:general_class_err} to design a sampling scheme specifically for classification, minimising mean classification loss rather than the common mean reconstruction loss objective. We propose greedily choosing $\set{S}$ to optimize \eqref{eq:gen_class_err} from Theorem \ref{thm:general_class_err} and we detail this sampling scheme in Algorithm \ref{alg:class_opt_samp}.

\renewcommand{\algorithmicrequire}{\textbf{Input:}}
\renewcommand{\algorithmicensure}{\textbf{Output:}}
\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Classification-Optimal Sampling}\label{alg:class_opt_samp}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]

\Require $m:$ Sample size;
\Statex \hspace*{\algorithmicindent - 1em} $\sigma:$ Noise standard deviation; 
 \Statex \hspace*{\algorithmicindent - 1em} $f:\, \mathbb{R}^{N \times d} \to \mathbb{R}^{N}: $ linear function s.t. the target classifier is $\matr{X} \mapsto \text{sign}(f(\matr{X}))$;
 \Statex \hspace*{\algorithmicindent - 1em} $\matr{G}, \vect{w}:$ Linear classifier parameters defining $f(\matr{X}) = \matr{G}\matr{X}\vect{w}$;
%\Require $\matr{G}, \vect{w}:$ Matrix and vector s.t. $f(\matr{X}) = \matr{G}\matr{X}\vect{w}$, \Require $R:$ Reconstruction method s.t. $R(\set{S}) = \matr{R}_{\set{S}}$
 \Statex \hspace*{\algorithmicindent - 1em} $\matr{C}:$ $\text{Cov}(\matr{X}\vect{w})(\sqnormvec{\vect{w}})^{-1}$ which is $\text{Cov}((\matr{X}^{T})_{1})$ if the columns of $\matr{X}$ are i.i.d.
\Ensure Vertex Sample Set $\set{S} \subseteq \set{N}$ of size $m$
\iffalse
\If{Columns of $\matr{X}$ are i.i.d.}
    \State $\matr{C} \gets \matr{\Sigma}$
\Else
    \State $\matr{C} \gets \text{Cov}(\matr{X}\vect{w})(\sqnormvec{\vect{w}})^{-1}$
\EndIf
\fi
\State $\matr{C}_{noisy} \gets \matr{C} + \sigma^{2}\matr{I}_{N}$
\State $\set{S} \gets \varnothing$
%\State $\set{V}_{rem} \gets \set{N}$
\State $\vect{\phi}^{2} \gets \text{diag}(\matr{G}\matr{C}\matr{G}^{T})$
\While {$|\set{S}| < m$}
    \State $l^{*} \leftarrow +\infty$
    \For{each $v \in \set{N} \,\backslash\, \set{S}$}
        \State $\matr{H}_{\set{S} \cup v} \gets \matr{G}R(\set{S} \cup v)$ \label{alg:defn_H_S}
        \State $\vect{c} \gets \text{diag}(\matr{H}_{\set{S} \cup v}\msubgen[\set{S} \cup v,\set{N}]{\matr{C}}\matr{G}^{T})$
        \State $\vect{\nu}^{2} \gets \text{diag}(\matr{H}_{\set{S} \cup v}\msubgen[\set{S} \cup v]{\matr{C}_{noisy}}\matr{H}^{T})$
        \For{$1 \leq i \leq N$}
        \State $\vect{\rho}_{i} \gets \frac{\vect{c}_{i}}{\sqrt{\vect{\phi}^{2}_{i}\vect{\nu}^{2}_{i}}}$
        \EndFor
        \State $l \gets \sum_{1 \leq i \leq N} \frac{1}{\pi}\text{arccos}(\vect{\rho}_{i})$
        \Comment{The classification loss for $\set{S} \cup v$}
        \If{$  l <  l*$}
            \State $l^* \gets l$
            \State $v^* \leftarrow v$
        \EndIf
    \EndFor
    \State $\set{S} \leftarrow \set{S} \cup \{v^*\}$
    %\State $\set{V}_{rem} \leftarrow \set{V}_{rem} \setminus \{v^*\}$
\EndWhile
\State \Return $\set{S}$
\end{algorithmic}

\subsection{Relations to previous work}
We now discuss our work in relation to Gama et al. \cite{gama2020rethinking} and Chamon et al. \cite{chamon2017greedy}, the main works in the GSP literature focusing on downstream tasks in the context of sampling. We first survey their theoretical results. Gama et al. focus on designing efficient joint sampling and reconstruction schemes under a linear reconstruction task represented by a matrix $\matr{H}$. They seek to construct an optimal `sketching operator' $\matr{H}_{\set{S}}$ which, when applied to the noisy observations, most closely approximates $\matr{H}$ applied to the true signal. From \cite[Proposition 2]{gama2020rethinking}, we can view their sketching operator as a combination of applying $\matr{H}$ after applying a reconstruction operator which they express as $\matr{R}_{X}\matr{C}^{T}\left(\matr{C}(\matr{R}_{X} + \matr{R}_{w})\matr{C}^{T}\right)^{-1}$, which in our notation would be $\msubgen[\set{N},\set{S}]{\text{Cov}(\vect{x})}\msubgen[\set{S}]{\text{Cov}(\matr{x}) + \text{Cov}(\matr{w})}^{-1}$. We mirror this definition in Line \ref{alg:defn_H_S} of our algorithm.  This \cite[Proposition 2]{gama2020rethinking}, along with \cite[(17) \& (18)]{gama2020rethinking} can be seen as a version of our Theorem \ref{thm:general_class_err} for linear downstream tasks. \iffalse We note that their \cite[Proposition 2]{gama2020rethinking} capture\fi Chamon et al. \cite{chamon2017greedy}, on the other hand, focus on greedy sampling and submodularity; similarly to Gama et al., they both optimize the sampling algorithm and the reconstruction operator. While their theoretical results do incorporate a linear `transform', understanding the impact of this transform, or downstream task, is not the focus of their work, and so analysis in this direction is limited.

One core difference between our work and that of Gama et al. and Chamon et al. is the setting. They focus on linear downstream tasks -- regression tasks -- such as estimating Gaussian Fourier coefficients and linear SVMs under a custom reconstruction operator. In contrast, we consider classification tasks via a sign nonlinearity, requiring more complex analysis (see Lemma \ref{lemma:gaussian_sign}) but also allowing us to directly contrast reconstruction and classification error with theoretical tools (see Corollary \ref{corr:relationship}). Both our work and Gama et al. apply their respective theoretical results empirically to a multi-class classification problem. This reflects the different goals of each of the works; we seek to understand the impact of downstream tasks on choices around sampling and reconstruction, focusing on the relationship between classification loss and reconstruction loss as sample size varies. Handling the non-linearity is therefore essential to our theoretical framework, as is using established reconstruction operators such as LS and FP. We analyse classification error and generate a sampling scheme specialised to classification in order to show how important considering the downstream task when sampling and reconstructing is. By contrast, Gama et al. sought to design an efficient sampling and reconstruction scheme; their results emphasise implementation efficiency e.g., \cite[Proposition 3]{gama2020rethinking} and  do not include the same theoretical analysis as ours. Similarly, Chamon et al. focus on analysis on the optimality of greedy sampling, and thus do not interpret the impact of their linear downstream task on their theoretical results, except briefly before \cite[Remark 2]{chamon2017greedy} and for the specific task of kernel PCA.

\subsubsection{Real-world Datasets}
We consider an FMRI dataset with nodes corresponding to brain regions and 292 graph signals corresponding to blood oxygen levels \cite{zhi2023gaussian, sripathmanathan2024impact}, subsampling a connected graph with $N=367$ nodes using neighbourhood sampling \cite{hamilton2017inductive}. We construct a classification task by subtracting the mean from all signals and taking the sign as node class labels, representing high or low blood oxygen levels.

We also consider the CoRA and Citeseer datasets, which are multi-class classification datasets. We take the largest connected component, bandlimit the features to each have bandwidth $k=\left\lfloor \frac{N}{10} \right\rfloor$, and construct $f$ by training an SGC (structured as in \eqref{eq:SGC_structure}, except $\matr{W}_{readout} \in \mathbb{R}^{32 \times C}$, where $C$ is the number of possible classes) using cross-entropy loss with bandlimited features and original labels. 
On these datasets we then evaluate `multi-class loss', between the original bandlimited signal and the reconstructed signal, which is:
\begin{align}
    &\text{Multi-Class Classification Loss} = {\sum_{i \in \set{V}}\prob{\text{argmax}\left(f(\matr{X})_{i}\right) \neq \text{argmax}(f(\hat{\matr{X}})_{i})}}
\end{align}
where for each node $i$, $f(\matr{X})_{i} \in \mathbb{R}^{C}$ and argmax yields the component with the largest value in $f(\matr{X})_{i}$.

These two setups each demonstrate different deviations of real world data from our synthetic setup -- the features in the FMRI do not necessarily match our bandlimited signal with additive noise signal model; however, we construct the rest of the task to match our synthetic setup. On the other hand, we bandlimit the features of the CoRA and Citeseer datasets, but change the task from a binary classifictaion to a multi-class classification task. This allows us to separately observe how these aspects of real world tasks interact with our theoretical results and proposed sampling scheme.

\subsection{Experimental Validation}
We experimentally validate our derivation of expected classification loss for different graph models, noise levels, reconstruction methods and versions of $f$. We note that both versions of $f$ can be written in the form $\matr{G}\matr{X}\vect{w}$, as required by Theorem \ref{thm:general_class_err}. Subfigures (b) and (c) of Figs. \ref{subfig:BA_noiseless_simple_LS}-\ref{subfig:SBM_noiseless_sgc_LS_lpinv} display the empirical loss calculations via directly reconstructing signals, and the analytic loss calculations using Theorem \ref{thm:general_class_err} respectively. We see that the empirical results match the analytic derivations in all cases, both in terms of magnitude and in terms of comparison between sampling schemes. We note that this is most evident in the simple case, where we have been able to use more signals than the SGC case owing to computational limitations; similarly, while Figs. \ref{subsubfig:SBM_noiseless_sgc_LS_class_anal_lpinv} and \ref{subsubfig:SBM_noiseless_sgc_LS_class_emp_lpinv} are not as clearly the same, we believe this is because of the greater variance in this setting, which can be seen by the confidence intervals being broader than in other settings. We also see that under \bs{noiseless} LS reconstruction (Figs. \ref{subfig:BA_noiseless_simple_LS}, \ref{subfig:BA_noiseless_sgc_LS} and \ref{subfig:SBM_noiseless_sgc_LS}), our classification loss looks like $x \to \arccos\left(\sqrt{x}\right)$, which follows the theoretical interpretation in Section \ref{sec:Gen_Theory}.

We now look at the reconstruction loss pictures in each of the figures. We first discuss the cases where $\matr{\Sigma} = \projbl$. Figs. \ref{subsubfig:BA_noiseless_simple_LS_rec}, \ref{subsubfig:BA_noiseless_sgc_LS_rec} and \ref{subsubfig:SBM_noiseless_sgc_LS_rec} are all set in the noiseless bandlimited signal with LS reconstruction setting, and all show a linear decrease in reconstruction error, as derived in Chapter \ref{ch:proj1_LS} (specifically presented in Appendix \ref{app:table_delta_proof}, Lemma \ref{lemma:LS_xi_1_is_rank}). Fig. \ref{subsubfig:BA_noisy_sgc_LS_rec} shows the case of a noisy bandlimited signal with LS reconstruction, and shows the characteristic spike when the sample size is the bandwidth derived in Chapter \ref{ch:proj1_LS}. In the case of Fig. \ref{subsubfig:BA_noiseless_sgc_feat_prop_rec}, while FP can be understood as GLR with $\mu \to 0$, the noise level is too low to encounter the phenomena characterised in Chapter \ref{ch:proj1_GLR}. Instead we see that the error is very high for very low sample sizes and then drops dramatically, decreasing to 0 as we move towards full observation.

We now discuss the cases where $\matr{\Sigma} = \matr{L}^{\dagger}$. In Figs. \ref{subsubfig:BA_noiseless_simple_LS_rec_lpinv}, \ref{subsubfig:BA_noiseless_sgc_LS_rec_lpinv}, \ref{subsubfig:BA_noisy_sgc_LS_rec_lpinv} and \ref{subsubfig:SBM_noiseless_sgc_LS_rec_lpinv}, we have LS reconstruction with an assumed bandwidth of $50$, and we see a spike when the sample size is equal to the assumed bandwidth. While the noiseless cases are not explicitly covered by the theory of Chapter \ref{ch:proj1_LS}, we note that this spike is the same as in the noisy bandlimited case, and thus it is likely the higher frequency parts of the signal are acting as `noise' in the reconstruction. Finally, in Fig. \ref{subsubfig:BA_noiseless_sgc_feat_prop_rec_lpinv}, we see error decreasing monotonically towards zero as sample size increases .


\subsection{Optimal Sampling}
\label{sec:optimal_sampling_results}
In this section, we discuss the impact of sampling on classification error, including our optimal sampling method. 
\paragraph{Simple setting, bandlimited signal}We begin by examining the experiments in the $f_{simple}$ setting under $\matr{\Sigma}=\projbl$. Fig. \ref{subsubfig:BA_noiseless_simple_LS_class_anal} shows all sample schemes having classification error decrease as sample size decreases, down to zero at a sample size equal to bandwidth, corresponding to how noiseless bandlimited signals can be perfectly reconstructed at this sample size -- and thus any function of the signal will be perfectly reconstructed at this sample size as well.  Fig. \ref{subsubfig:BA_noiseless_simple_LS_class_anal} shows that A-Optimal sampling (`reconstruction-optimal') is consistently worse than random sampling, and that our classification optimal sampling improves on both. We find experimentally this is because of the mismatch in the objectives; A-Optimal sampling chooses vertices which increase the standard deviation of $c_{i}(\matr{I})$ -- this can be viewed as learning about high variance nodes rather than broadly learning about every node. Random sampling has a lower standard deviation of $c_{i}(\matr{I})$, with classification-optimal sampling having significantly lower standard deviation of $c_{i}(\matr{I})$ compared to the other schemes. This corresponds to the intuition that under classification, knowledge about specific nodes is less valuable as they can only contribute a maximum of 1 to the loss, compared to under reconstruction where a node which is significantly wrong can signficantly negatively impact reconstruction error. This incentivises a classification-optimal scheme to try and pick nodes which will be broadly informative about every node, while reconstruction-optimal schemes are less even in their choice. This emphasises how sampling schemes need to be designed in light of the downstream task.

\paragraph{SGC setting, bandlimited signal}In the noiseless $f_{SGC}$ setting under LS reconstruction, Fig. \ref{subsubfig:BA_noiseless_sgc_LS_class_anal} shows the same patterns as we described for Figs. \ref{subsubfig:BA_noiseless_simple_LS_class_anal} and \ref{subsubfig:BA_noiseless_sgc_feat_prop_class_anal_lpinv} above -- we see that our novel classification-optimal method outperforms both A-optimal sampling and random sampling. While we cannot perform the same analysis as above, because $c_{i}(\matr{G}) \neq c_{i}(\matr{I})$ and $\phi_{i}(\matr{G}) \neq \phi_{i}(\matr{I})$, the similarity of the results suggests our above analysis of sampling methods in the $f_{simple}$ case carries over to the $f_{SGC}$ case. 

In the FP reconstruction case (Fig. \ref{subsubfig:BA_noiseless_sgc_feat_prop_class_anal}), we see that both forms of optimal sampling are approximately equal, with both beating random sampling significantly, and all schemes converging to zero classification error at full observation. We do see that reconstruction-optimal sampling marginally beats classification sampling under reconstruction loss, and vice-versa for classification loss.

In the noisy case (Fig. \ref{subsubfig:BA_noisy_sgc_LS_class_anal}), we see classification-optimal sampling has lower classification loss except for sample sizes near the bandwidth (marked with the vertical dashed line); near the bandwidth, the impact of noise is the highest for LS \cite{sripathmanathan2024impact} and thus reconstruction-optimal sampling, which optimizes for noise sensitivity more aggressively, has both lower reconstruction and classification loss. This is an artefact of the greedy construction of our sampling sets -- our objective for classification loss captures the impact of noise, and thus an exhaustive search optimising over all possible sample sets under our loss would outperform in this noisy classification compared to (exhaustive) A-optimal sampling. However, the importance of choosing nodes to decrease sensitivity to noise only becomes pertinent to the objective nearer the bandwidth, and our sampling scheme struggles to 'catch-up' on this front in comparison to A-optimal sampling, which had been choosing sample sets which are robust to noise all along.  

\paragraph{$\matr{L}^{\dagger}$ signal} In this setting, we group our analysis of the simple setting with the SGC setting. We first note that Figs. \ref{subsubfig:BA_noiseless_simple_LS_class_anal_lpinv}, \ref{subsubfig:BA_noiseless_sgc_LS_class_anal_lpinv} and \ref{subsubfig:BA_noisy_sgc_LS_class_anal_lpinv}, corresponding to the noiseless simple, noiseless SGC and noisy SGC settings under LS reconstruction settings, look very similar under $\matr{\Sigma} = \matr{L}^{\dagger}$, once again suggesting that the high-frequency component of the signal is acting as noise. In these cases we see a classification error dropping before briefly rising near a sample size of 50 (corresponding to the assumed bandwidth of the LS reconstruction) before dropping again, levelling out between 100 and 150; this relatively high classification error even at full sample size demonstrates how the difference in signal model assumption can be significant in the classification setting. The peak mirrors the `medium noise' experiments in Chapter \ref{ch:proj1_LS}, for example the pattern we see in Fig. \ref{BA_MSE_subfigb} under weighted random sampling, as the sensitivity to noise ($\Delta_{2}$) of LS increases close to assumed bandwidth; our experiments suggest a similar phenomenon under classification. In contrast to the noiseless bandlimited signal case, we see that both reconstruction-optimal and classification-optimal sampling improve upon random sampling in this setting, with the difference between classification- and reconstruction- optimal sampling decreasing as sample size increases to the assumed bandwidth, then marginally increasing again before decreasing as all sampling methods converge as sample size tends to full observation. The main differences between the simple setting (Fig. \ref{subsubfig:BA_noiseless_simple_LS_class_anal_lpinv}) and the SGC setting (Figs. \ref{subsubfig:BA_noiseless_sgc_LS_class_anal_lpinv} and \ref{subsubfig:BA_noisy_sgc_LS_class_anal_lpinv}) are the intensity of the peak, the difference between sampling schemes and the classification error at full observation. We note that in the simple case, the peak is less pronounced, the differences in classification error between sample schemes is smaller, and the classification error at full observation is higher.

A similar, but distinct pattern occurs in under ths SBM graph model in Fig. \ref{subsubfig:SBM_noiseless_sgc_LS_class_anal_lpinv}; we see a similar peak pattern as in Figs. \ref{subsubfig:BA_noiseless_sgc_LS_class_anal_lpinv} and \ref{subsubfig:BA_noisy_sgc_LS_class_anal_lpinv}. However, the impact of classification- and reconstruction-optimal sampling in the SBM model compared to random sampling is reduced compared to the BA graph model. Furthermore, much like in the bandlimited signal case (Fig. \ref{subsubfig:SBM_noiseless_sgc_LS_class_anal}), we see classification-optimal sampling noticeably improving on reconstruction-optimal sampling at sample sizes below bandwidth. However, we also see that reconstruction-optimal sampling is better at sample sizes very close to bandwidth, which we hypothesise, like before, is because of the impact of greediness being a heuristic optimisation, and thus not being able to optimise for the objective as noise sensitivity becomes increasingly important close to the sample size of 50. Overall, we find out classification-sampling scheme has the most advantage in SBM graphs.

Finally, we discuss the results under FP reconstruction. We note that all sample schemes tend to zero classification error as the sample size tends to full observation, which is expected as FP does not smooth the signal on observed nodes, and the signal is noiseless. Here, both classification- and reconstruction-optimal sampling outperform random sampling under classification error; interestingly, our classification-optimal scheme does not noticeably outperform reconstruction-optimal sampling until sample sizes above 250; this improvement in performance at large sample sizes is in contrast to Fig \ref{subsubfig:SBM_noiseless_sgc_LS_class_anal}, where our classification-optimal sampling scheme shows the most value over reconstruction-optimal sampling at relatively smaller sample sizes.

\paragraph{Real world datasets}
In our real-world fMRI dataset (Fig. \ref{fig:fmri}) -- which does not have bandlimited features -- we find, for classification loss, both reconstruction and classification-optimal sampling outperforming random sampling, with our novel classification-optimal sampling scheme marginally outperforming at sample sizes over 90. Both reconstruction- and classification-optimal sampling gain little benefit at sample sizes over 100.

For CoRA, we have bandlimited our signal, and thus see perfect reconstruction at sample sizes equal to the bandwidth, with reconstruction error decreasing to zero as the sample size increases until being equal to the bandwidth which is 248, and with numeric error being captured in Fig \ref{subfig:cora_LS_rec} at sample sizes larger than 248. In Fig. \ref{subfig:cora_LS_class}, we see classification error decreasing to zero as sample size increases to 248 for all sample selection methods, and we see both random sampling and classification-optimal sampling significantly outperforming reconstruction-optimal sampling, with classification-optimal sampling being the best by a noticeable margin. This experiment shows that even though we may have assumed binary classification and independent features to compute our sampling scheme, it can also be applied to similar downstream tasks like multi-class classification.



\section{Discussion}
In this Chapter we have studied how classification differs from reconstruction in the presence of noise and missing data. We have given theoretical and empirical characterisation of the relationship between classification and reconstruction error, where classification is done by a linearized GCN. We have shown that reconstruction-optimal sampling can underperform random sampling in the context of a downstream task. We have then presented a novel optimal sampling scheme specifically for classification with a GCN, and showed its performance across multiple graph models, reconstruction methods and noise levels. The underperformance of reconstruction-optimal sampling versus random sampling and the superior performance of our proposed algorithm both demonstrate the importance of taking downstream tasks into account in the context of sampling and reconstruction.
