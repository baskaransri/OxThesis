\chapter{\label{ch:conclusion}Conclusion}

\section{Summary}
In this thesis, we have provided theoretical and empirical results to help understand incomplete information in the context of learning on graphs. We briefly summarise our contributions.

In Chapter \ref{ch:proj1_LS}, we demonstrated that reconstruction error was not monotonic in sample size for LS, an unregularised method. We showed that optimal sampling as currently understood in the literature cannot mitigate this non-monotonicity, and that under such `optimal' methods at high noise levels the reconstruction error must be $\Lambda$-shaped with a peak at the bandwidth of the signal.  This demonstrates some of the complexity underlying understanding the impact of sample size on reconstruction error, where increasing sample size can \emph{increase} error at high noise levels, even under optimal sampling schemes. We derive our results for multiple noise models and all signal models (under loose constraints).

In Chapter \ref{ch:proj1_GLR} we significantly increase the breadth of our settings. We construct a framework for bounding the MSE of GLR in terms of graph properties, a contribution in and of itself. We show the non-monotonicity of reconstruction error with sample size under a regularised reconstruction method and high noise; it is a much more natural setting to consider regularised reconstruction methods under high noise, thus significantly strengthening the applicability of our non-monotonicity claims. Our results in this chapter hold for any sample set of a fixed size without repeated observations, thus showing significant limitations in trying to handle noise with sample selection schemes which do not repeatedly observe any vertex. We also present a novel variant of Kantorovich's inequality for bandlimited noise in Appendix \ref{app:2-proj1}, a powerful tool for variance analysis under graph-related noise.

In Chapter \ref{ch:proj2} we extend our analysis of missing data to the case of a downstream task, specifically classification under a linearised GNN.  We give an exact analytic characterisation under Gaussian assumptions, and use this to derive a sampling scheme for classification. We show the impact of the downstream task by experimentally showing cases where reconstruction-optimal sampling underperforms random sampling for classification, with our novel sampling scheme outperforming both.

All of our theoretical results have been accompanied by extensive empirical validation.

Overall, we have thoroughly investigated different aspects of learning on graphs with incomplete information, questioning key implicit assumptions in the field and building theoretical frameworks to better understand how and why these assumptions might fail.

\section{Limitations \& Future Work}
In this subsection, we list potential future directions. We consider future work inspired by limitations in our settings, limitations in our approach and finally by limitations in the broader literature.

\subsection{Limitation in our setting \& future work}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Impact of sample size on non-optimally regularised LS} Our work in Chapter \ref{ch:proj1_GLR} presents the impact of sample size on a common regularised reconstruction method, GLR. However, regularised variants of LS are also used in the GSP literature, such as Optimal Bayesian Reconstruction \cite{chamon2017greedy,li2024finding}. Optimal Bayesian Reconstruction requires perfect knowledge of noise levels for optimality to hold, and thus in practice is rarely actually optimal; it could be fruitful to extend our analysis to non-optimally regularised LS and in doing so see how sensitive the monotonicity of reconstruction error with sample size is to the assumption of exact knowledge of noise in Optimal Bayesian Reconstruction.
    \item \textbf{Bounds for GLR under other graph Laplacians} Our work in Chapter \ref{ch:proj1_GLR} relies on the fact that the eigenvector of the combinatorial Laplacian that corresponds to the zero eigenvalue is $\vect{1}$ in order to construct a bound which does not depend on the sample set. Future work could relax this constraint and explore similar bounds for a normalised graph Laplacian.
    \item \textbf{Divergent signal models} In Chapters \ref{ch:proj1_LS} \& \ref{ch:proj1_GLR} our analysis focuses on the noise model to allow for broad applicability, looking at the underexplored $\xi_{2}(\set{S})$, with much less analysis on $\xi_{1}(\set{S})$. However, in Chapter \ref{ch:proj2}, Fig. \ref{subsubfig:BA_noiseless_simple_LS_rec_lpinv} we see that divergence between the assumed signal model of LS and the true signal model of $\matr{L}^{\dagger}$ causes noise-like behaviour (i.e., non-monotonicity of reconstruction error as sample size increases). We briefly mention in Chapter \ref{ch:proj1_GLR} this noiseless non-monotonicity can occur with GLR as well. Extension of the results in Chapters \ref{ch:proj1_LS} \& \ref{ch:proj1_GLR} to cover the impact of $\xi_{1}$ in cases like this as sample size varies would complete the analysis on MSE, allowing for characterisation of non-monotonicity in the noiseless case as well.
    \item \textbf{Theoretical analysis multi-class classification} In Chapter \ref{ch:proj2}, our theoretical analysis covers binary classification as a downstream task, but not multi-class classification, which is a common setting in publically available graph datasets such as CoRA.
    \item \textbf{Unknown graphs} Our analysis in all chapters assumes a known graph. Chapter \ref{ch:proj1_LS} only requires the graph being unchanging and Chapter \ref{ch:proj1_GLR} relies on bounds based on Laplacian eigenvalues which are relatively stable under perturbation. However, Chapter \ref{ch:proj2} proposes a sampling scheme which is dependent on the underlying graph, and it is future work to understand this dependency and what happens if the graph is not known.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Limitations in our approach \& future work}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bounds on $\xi_{2}$ under LS} In Chapter \ref{ch:proj1_LS}, we only present relatively trivial lower bounds on the noise-sensitivity term $\xi_{2}$ for LS, unlike in Chapter \ref{ch:proj1_GLR} for GLR. Tight analytic bounds could help in the same way they help in Chapter \ref{ch:proj1_GLR} -- by relating properties of the graph to the noise sensitivity of reconstruction.
    \item \textbf{Early-Stopping Sampling Schemes} Chapters \ref{ch:proj1_LS} \& \ref{ch:proj1_GLR} show how increasing sample size can decrease performance, suggesting exploration of the early stopping schemes for sampling which we briefly outline in the respective discussion sections.
    \item \textbf{Downstream task bounds} In Chapter \ref{ch:proj2}, Theorem \ref{thm:general_class_err} explicitly calculates the classification error in terms of $\rho_{i}(\matr{G})$. While such a formula is useful for calculation, it is not particularly revealing in terms of understanding the impact the graph has on sample choice under classification error. Such a bound could help understand why classification-optimal sampling has more of an advantage over random sampling in graph models with community structure such as SBM as compared to graph models such as BA.
    \item \textbf{Classification loss for a broader class of GNNs} In Chapter \ref{ch:proj2}, Theorem \ref{thm:general_class_err} our formulation requires linearity of $f$, restricting the class of graph models we can analyse. Extension of this result to cover even a single non-linearity in $f$ would expand the number of GNNs this theory covers.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Limitations in the literature \& future work}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Deterministic sampling schemes with repetition} Our results in Chapters \ref{ch:proj1_LS} \& \ref{ch:proj1_GLR} shows a limitation of current optimality criteria in the literature; at high noise levels, observing more new nodes can be actively harmful to reconstruction performance. Instead, we suggest sampling schemes in the literature should consider repeated observations of nodes if robustness to noise is a goal.
    \item \textbf{Entries to Laplacian eigenvectors \& nodal domains} Sampling under LS and bandlimited signals is intimately related to the entries of the eigenvectors of the Laplacian, however the literature does not make use of results in other fields on this front. Relevant work includes work on Nodal Domains (the zeros of eigenvectors) \cite{biyikougu2007laplacian} and results related to determinants of submatrices of the Laplacian \cite{denton2022eigenvectors}.
    \item \textbf{Stochastic Blockmodel and Concentration of Measure} The Stochastic Blockmodel is a common model in GSP, and the Contextual SBM has gained popularity in recent years for analysis of GNNs \cite{shi2024homophily, zheng2024missing}. A concentration of measure based approach is both rigorous and effective in the case of the SBM, being used to derive limit theorems for Laplacian eigenvectors and thus make rigorous claims about spectral clustering \cite{tang2018limit}. A broader adoption of this tool in the GSP literature and standardisation of notation around it could allow for faster rigorous iteration when considering GNNs on CSBM models.
    \item \textbf{Formalisation of minibatch sampling via GSP} Most mini-batch sampling papers in the GML literature use theory, but lack the theoretical rigour the GSP literature holds itself to. A formalisation of mini-batch sampling from the ground up in a GSP context could allow us to use powerful statistical tools such as Kantorovich's inequality with the knowledge the assumptions of the tools hold.
    \item \textbf{Manifold symmetries for graph sampling} Much work relates the graph setting to the manifold setting, with parallel literature on graph and manifold learning and data reconstruction, and links via treating graphs as discretisations of manifolds. Indeed, equivariant GNNs have made steps forward in physics-driven applications. However, these data symmetries have not yet been exploited in the context of graph sampling, and this has implication for sampling for physics applications.
    \item \textbf{Distributional assumptions around missing data} The GSP literature on missing data tends to assume that data is missing at random, with potentially a few works covering adversarial data loss. Data loss however, may correlate with the data itself -- consider temperature sensors, where high temperatures may cause issues in the surrounding area, disabling sensors. A proposal for such a model and analysis in the graph setting could have multiple practical applications in sensors.
\end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Should the inferred data match the missing data?} Work in `rewiring' in GNNs separates out the notion of a computational graph and the actual data graph, noting that the true graph may not lead to the best inference because of limitations elsewhere (e.g. GNNs struggling with bottlenecks \cite{barbero2024localityaware}). The current models of handling missing data in GSP tend to think about explicit or implicit reconstruction of the data; can one use GSP to instead understand learning the optimal representation for a task in light of missing features without reconstructing? 
    \item \textbf{When is the data insufficient?} In this thesis, we have noted specific cases where the data is insufficient -- noise levels at which throwing away all of the data gives a better outcome on average than using it. Given a downstream task, when is a trivial non-data driven estimator better than a data driven one? How does this limit look as the downstream task changes?
    \item \textbf{Heuristics and Theory} Part of the work of engineering is reckoning with the fact that we cannot perfectly model reality; that our theoretical models will depend on assumptions which may not perfectly hold and that we will need to apply some model regardless, using the tools available to us.
    Our work in this thesis, especially Chapters \ref{ch:proj1_LS} \& \ref{ch:proj1_GLR}, highlights how an assumption which seems `reasonable' and is proven in multiple simplified models, can be false. Is this a fundamental flaw in trying to extend theory heuristically? 
    \item \textbf{Is work on missing data moral?} This thesis outlines ways of handling missing data, which can be excluded for privacy reasons. As techniques for inference improve, we can accurately predict things that people may not want us to know, and act on them in ways which could be harmful -- an infamous example being the supermarket Target sending ads for pregnancy-related items to a woman living with her father who did not know about the pregnancy and may have disapproved \cite{charles2015companies}. Graph-related methods have obvious potential for both great help, e.g., with regards to drug discovery, and great harm, e.g., with regards to inference of private data via social networks, and it is incumbent upon us researchers to understand the potential impact of our work on society, and each weigh up the impact we want to have on the world.
\end{itemize}
